> publishers; always follow the required format where one exists.

## What a disclosure is for

A disclosure lets the reader understand how the work was produced. It is not a
confession and not a formality — it is information the assessor or reader needs
to interpret the work correctly.

## The three elements

A useful disclosure names:

1. **The tool** — what you used ("ChatGPT", "a general-purpose LLM assistant").
2. **The task** — what you used it for, specifically.
3. **The boundary** — what remained your own work.

## Worked examples

**Good — specific and bounded:**

> I used ChatGPT to generate an initial outline of possible essay structures.
> I selected and reorganised one structure, and wrote all of the text myself.
> No AI-generated text appears in the submission.

**Good — editing use:**

> I wrote the full draft myself, then used an AI assistant to suggest
> grammar and phrasing improvements, which I reviewed and applied selectively.
> The analysis and conclusions are unchanged from my draft.

**Poor — too vague to be useful:**

> AI was used in preparing this assignment.

**Poor — undermines authorship:**

> I asked the AI to write each section and edited the result.

The last example is honest, which matters — but it discloses substitution, not
assistance. If your accurate disclosure would look like this, the problem is
the working method, not the wording.

## Rules of thumb

- Write the disclosure at the moment you use the tool, not from memory at
  submission time.
- Specific beats long: two accurate sentences beat a paragraph of hedging.
- If you are unsure whether something needs disclosing, disclose it.
