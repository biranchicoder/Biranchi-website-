## Evidence considered

- The submitted essay and its drafting history, showing substantial original
  composition over several sessions.
- The unit's assessment instructions and AI-use declaration requirement.
- The student's account: the outline structured the essay, but every paragraph
  was written by the student.

## Reasoning

Applying the decision framework in order: AI use of this kind **was permitted**
for planning, so the use itself is not a breach. The substantive intellectual
work — argument, evidence, prose — **was the student's own**, and authorship is
preserved: the student can explain and defend the essay. The breach is narrow
and specific: the required **disclosure was not made**. That is a genuine
policy breach, but of the acknowledgement rule, not of authorship or honesty
about the work's content.

## Decision

**Disclosure problem.** The appropriate response addresses the acknowledgement
failure — for example an educative outcome or a penalty tied to the declaration
requirement — not a contract-cheating response.

## Alternative interpretation

A stricter reading could treat any undisclosed AI use as dishonesty per se,
reasoning that the declaration exists precisely so markers know how work was
produced. On that view the case edges toward misconduct. This reading is
defensible but disproportionate where the underlying use was permitted and the
work is authentically the student's.

## Confidence

**High.** The facts are clear, the applicable rule is explicit, and the only
contested question is proportionality of response.

## Procedural fairness considerations

- The student should be told exactly which rule was breached (disclosure, not
  authorship) and shown the declaration requirement.
- The outcome should not be recorded or communicated as contract cheating.
- The student should have the opportunity to explain why disclosure was missed
  (e.g. unclear instructions), which is relevant to penalty.
