## Evidence considered

- The submission, the student's declaration describing grammar-level AI
  assistance, and a pre-editing draft supplied by the student.
- Comparison of the draft and final versions, showing sentence-level polish
  with no change to structure, argument or evidence.

## Reasoning

Every question in the decision framework resolves in the student's favour: the
use **was permitted**, it **was disclosed** exactly as required, AI **assisted
rather than replaced** the work (the analysis and argument pre-existed the AI
step), the student reviewed the edited text before submitting, **authorship is
fully preserved**, and there is **no deception** — the declaration accurately
describes what happened.

## Decision

**Acceptable.** This is the policy working as intended: transparent, bounded
assistance that improves communication without displacing the student's
intellectual contribution.

## Alternative interpretation

One could ask whether heavy language editing disadvantages assessment of
writing skill where written expression is itself a learning outcome. If the
rubric explicitly assesses independent language proficiency, the marker may
need to weigh that criterion differently — but that is an assessment-design
question, not an integrity breach by the student, who followed the rules as
written.

## Confidence

**High.** Facts, rule and outcome align; the case is a model of compliant use.

## Procedural fairness considerations

- Students who disclose honestly must not be treated with more suspicion than
  students who stay silent; penalising disclosure teaches concealment.
- Any concern about language-proficiency outcomes should be addressed through
  assessment design and communicated in advance, not applied retrospectively.
